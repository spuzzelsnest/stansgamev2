The History - Pegasus Bridge. Narration, one line a shot. Make 01.mp3, 02.mp3 ... and drop them in this folder.

01  Before the invasion, RAF reconnaissance aircraft photograph every bridge in Normandy. Here: the bridge over the Caen Canal at Bénouville. The Germans are digging trenches and building a concrete pillbox to guard it.
02  Two bridges, a few hundred metres apart: over the canal, and over the River Orne. They carry the only road across. Whoever holds them can stop German tanks reaching the beaches - or let the British through.
03  Between the bridges: flat fields cut by long lines of trees. And next to the canal bridge, a small triangle of open field. The plan is to land gliders right there - in the dark.
04  The job goes to Major John Howard. A Londoner, he joined the army as a private, left to become a policeman in Oxford, came back when war broke out and became an officer. Now 31, he commands D Company of the Ox and Bucks Light Infantry.
05  For months he trains them, day and night, on bridges in England. On D-Day: about 180 men - D Company, two platoons of B Company and 30 Royal Engineers - in six Horsa gliders. Three for each bridge.
06  The fifth of June, 1944, four minutes to eleven at night. From RAF Tarrant Rushton in Dorset, six Halifax bombers tow the six Horsas into the dark. Ninety minutes across the sea. Behind them, the greatest invasion fleet the world has ever seen. Just after midnight, over the French coast, the gliders cast off - and the pilots fly by stopwatch and compass, down through the cloud, towards the canal.
07  On the bridge the German sentries walk their rounds. Most of the garrison is asleep. They hear nothing but the wind.
08  Glider 91 - Staff Sergeants Jim Wallwork and John Ainsworth - crashes through the wire just yards from the bridge. The pilots are thrown out, stunned. Everyone inside is alive.
09  Glider 92 lands behind it, then 93, which breaks its back on the edge of a pond. Private Fred Greenhalgh of glider 93 is killed in the landing. Nobody knows exactly how - he is usually said to have drowned in the pond.
10  25 Platoon (Brotheridge) rushes the bridge. 24 Platoon (Wood) clears the trenches on the east bank, 14 Platoon (Smith) follows over to the west bank. The sappers go over the bridge looking for the charges. In ten minutes it is over.
11  Lieutenant Den Brotheridge leads 25 Platoon straight across the bridge. The Germans are taken completely by surprise. Grenades go into the pillbox; the trenches on both banks are cleared.
12  Near the Café Gondrée, Den Brotheridge is shot in the neck. He dies about an hour later at the aid post - one of the first Allied soldiers killed by enemy fire on D-Day.
13  Lieutenant Sandy Smith of glider 93 has badly hurt his knee in the landing, but he runs over the bridge anyway, keeping to the side. At the far end a German throws a stick grenade: it tears his wrist open to the bone. Smith shoots the man as he climbs over the café wall.
14  By chance a German sergeant, Hickmann, drives up the road with a few young paratroopers. They see that the bridge has fallen, fire at it for a while, and slip away into the dark.
15  Captain Jock Neilson's sappers climb all over the bridge. The demolition charges are not in place: the Germans have not fitted them. Where are the explosives?
16  At the river: glider 96 (Fox) lands nearest. Sergeant Thornton's mortar hits the German machine gun with its first bomb, Fox charges, and the guards run. Glider 95 (Sweeney) lands further away and arrives to find it all over. Glider 94 was cast off over the wrong river. At 00:50 the paras drop east of the Orne; 7th Battalion hurries over both bridges into Bénouville. About 02:00 German armour comes down the road - Sergeant Thornton's PIAT stops the leading vehicle.
17  Both bridges are taken. Corporal Ted Tappenden sends the success signal on the wireless: "Ham and Jam" - over and over, for a long time before anybody answers.
18  A German car and a motorcyclist race over the bridges from the east - straight into the British guns. In the car is the commander of the bridge garrison, Major Hans Schmidt, wounded in the leg. He tells the doctor, Captain John Vaughan, that they will all be thrown back into the sea. A shot of morphine calms him down.
19  Georges and Thérèse Gondrée and their daughters, Georgette and Arlette, hid in their cellar. Their café is one of the first houses in France to be freed. Georges digs up the champagne he had buried in 1940 to keep it from the Germans.
20  In Bénouville, A Company of 7th Parachute Battalion holds the crossroads by the château. At first light German guns fire straight up the street and the paras fall back house by house. German infantry creep up through the château park; tanks come up the road. A half-track heading for the bridge is knocked out by the Mairie. A Company is cut off - but the Germans do not get through.
21  At the crossroads a tank comes within four yards of A Company's trench. Private Michael McGee walks out at it, firing his Bren from the hip, while two paras run in with Gammon bombs. Further back, Germans storm a house full of wounded men, and the battalion padre, George Parry, is killed defending them.
22  At the bridge, Wally Parr's men learn to fire a captured German anti-tank gun. One shot goes clean through the water tower, where snipers are hiding; another hits a tree by the château. Howard won't let them fire at the château itself: it is a maternity hospital.
23  A German plane drops a bomb right onto the bridge. It hits the steel with a clang, falls into the canal - and does not go off. Later the sappers find the explosives at last, stored in a shed near the bridge. The bridge was never ready to be blown.
24  From Sword Beach the commandos of Lord Lovat's 1st Special Service Brigade fight their way through, Piper Bill Millin playing. The airborne men and the men from the sea have met.
25  Lord Lovat shakes hands with Howard, and the commandos cross at the double. Lovat tells Bill Millin to keep playing all the way over the river bridge. Snipers fire from the trees and towers - some commandos are killed on the bridge - but Millin walks over, playing.
26  That night the 2nd Battalion of the Royal Warwickshire Regiment arrives, and Howard's men hand over the bridges. The canal bridge gets a new name: Pegasus Bridge, after the winged horse on the airborne soldiers' badge.
27  In the dark, Howard's men march to Ranville. There, out of the night, come the men of glider 94: they had landed by the River Dives, miles to the east, and found their own way back.
28  John Howard is awarded the Distinguished Service Order. Den Brotheridge lies in the churchyard at Ranville. The river bridge is named Horsa Bridge, after the gliders - and the Café Gondrée still stands beside Pegasus Bridge.
29  We remember. Lieutenant Den Brotheridge, 25 Platoon, 2nd Ox & Bucks.   Private Fred Greenhalgh, 14 Platoon, 2nd Ox & Bucks.   And the men of 7th Parachute Battalion, the commandos, the glider pilots and the aircrews who did not come home.
